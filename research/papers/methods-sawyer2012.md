# Sawyer et al. 2012 — how DNA damage and fragmentation change with age

- **Citation:** Sawyer S, Krause J, Guschanski K, Savolainen V, Pääbo S. (2012) Temporal patterns of nucleotide misincorporations and DNA fragmentation in ancient DNA. *PLoS ONE* 7:e34131.
- **DOI:** 10.1371/journal.pone.0034131 · PMC3316601 · **Verification:** full_text

## Claims we use
1. Outside permafrost, endogenous DNA in ancient remains is usually present in small amounts alongside much larger amounts of DNA from microbes that colonised the specimen after death. *(Introduction.)* [ev-sawyer2012-1]
2. Across 65 libraries from samples 18 to about 60,000 years old, median fragment length showed no significant correlation with age; samples under 100 years old could be as short as 44 bp. The authors infer that most fragmentation happens soon after death. *(Results; Fig 2; Discussion.)* [ev-sawyer2012-2]
3. The C-to-T rate at the first 5′ position rises strongly with age; leaving aside heated specimens, every sample younger than 100 years had under 10% and every sample older than 500 years had over 10%. *(Results; Fig 4; Discussion.)* [ev-sawyer2012-3]
4. Laboratory precautions cannot remove modern DNA already in a specimen when it arrives; heating raised C-to-T rates in some specimens, and bleach treatment may raise deamination in contaminating DNA that survives it. The authors suggest using the damage pattern together with controls and other checks for specimens not exposed to unusual treatment. *(Introduction; Discussion.)* [ev-sawyer2012-4]
5. Present-day human DNA often contaminates ancient bones and cannot easily be told apart, by its sequence, from the DNA of ancient humans. *(Introduction.)* [ev-sawyer2012-5]

## Notes
- Mostly mitochondrial DNA from museum animals plus Neandertal libraries; the 10% and 500-year figures come from this sample set.
