# Briggs et al. 2007 — patterns of damage in Neandertal DNA

- **Citation:** Briggs AW, Stenzel U, Johnson PLF, et al. (2007) Patterns of damage in genomic DNA sequences from a Neandertal. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA* 104:14616–14621.
- **DOI:** 10.1073/pnas.0704665104 · PMC1976210 · **Verification:** full_text

## Claims we use
1. In the Pleistocene specimens sequenced directly up to then (mammoth, cave bear, Neandertal), most retrieved sequences came from microorganisms that colonised the tissue after death, with only a fraction from the organism itself. *(Introduction.)* [ev-briggs2007-1]
2. The purines A and G are overrepresented just before strand breaks, suggesting that depurination (loss of a purine base, after which the backbone is prone to break) contributed to fragmentation; the authors estimate it explains on the order of 10% of breaks in their Neandertal sample. *(Abstract; Results: Ancient DNA Fragmentation.)* [ev-briggs2007-2]
3. C-to-T substitutions are greatly elevated and concentrated at the 5′ ends of molecules (about 21% of reference Cs read as T at the first position, falling over about 10 positions), with a matching G-to-A excess at 3′ ends in their double-stranded library protocol; other substitutions are rare. *(Results: Nucleotide Misincorporations; Fig 3.)* [ev-briggs2007-3]
4. Their model indicates that cytosine deamination is much faster in single-stranded overhangs at molecule ends: an estimated 68% (95% CI 65–71%) of cytosines in single-stranded ends versus 0.97% in double-stranded parts. *(Results: A Model of Ancient DNA Damage; Table 1.)* [ev-briggs2007-4]
5. The authors argue that all steps up to adapter ligation should be done in a laboratory dedicated to ancient DNA, and that project-specific adapters should be used so that contamination from other sources and libraries can be detected. *(Results and Discussion: Considerations for Genome Sequencing.)* [ev-briggs2007-5]
6. At the time, the authors argued that neither fragment length nor damage-type misincorporations efficiently separate endogenous from contaminating DNA, since contaminants can also be short and can carry similar changes; only sequence differences can positively identify contamination. *(Considerations for Genome Sequencing.)* [ev-briggs2007-6, stance: qualifies]

## Notes
- Claim 6 is an earlier position; later work (Sawyer et al. 2012; Skoglund et al. 2014) used damage to separate ancient from recent DNA. We show both.
